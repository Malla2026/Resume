Mobility—particularly road transportation—is vital to Nepal’s social and economic development as a landlocked country. However, growing concerns have emerged regarding the long-term sustainability of the nation’s transport system, especially due to rising dependence on imported fossil fuels (gasoline and diesel) and the resulting greenhouse gas emissions and local air pollution.
<br>
Over the past three decades, the number of registered motor vehicles in Nepal has increased dramatically, from approximately 76 thousand in 1990 to nearly 5 million in 2022 (Figure 1). This growth has been driven primarily by the rapid expansion of private two-wheelers. Consequently, demand for imported petroleum products has surged, placing increasing pressure on foreign currency reserves and raising concerns about national energy security. Between 1994 and 2022, combined gasoline and diesel imports increased eleven-fold (Figure 1), while the import value of these fuels rose nearly ten-fold—from NRs 26.7 billion in 2009 to NRs 252.7 billion in 2022 (MOCIS, 2024, NTIP data portal). In 2022 alone, the combined import value of gasoline and diesel accounted for more than 5% of the country’s GDP.
<br>
To address these challenges, Nepal has adopted favorable fiscal policies to promote electric vehicle (EV) imports through tax incentives. These policies aim to reduce petroleum imports, improve energy security, and lower greenhouse gas emissions and local air pollutants. However, such incentives also have economic implications for the government, particularly in terms of reduced tax revenue. If EV adoption continues to grow and tax revenues from vehicle imports decline further, the government may find it economically necessary to revise and potentially increase taxes on EVs in the future.
<br>
This raises two important policy questions. First, to what extent can EV tax rates be increased without discouraging consumers from choosing EVs over internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles? Second, even with higher taxes, do EVs remain cost-competitive with conventional ICE vehicles over their operational lifetime? Despite the recent increase in EV imports driven by tax incentives, many consumers remain hesitant to adopt EVs due to limited awareness of their long-term ownership costs, including both upfront purchase costs and ongoing operating expenses.
<br>
This study seeks to address these questions. First, it estimates the level of additional taxation on EVs that would bring their market prices closer to those of comparable ICE vehicles. Second, it evaluates and compares the lifetime ownership costs of EVs and ICE vehicles using two indicators: Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) and Levelized Cost of Driving (LCOD). These estimates are conducted under scenarios with and without increased EV taxation. The analysis focuses specifically on private and public passenger vehicles.
