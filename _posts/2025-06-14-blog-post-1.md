Industrial boilers are the primary source of thermal energy (steam or hot water) for a wide range of manufacturing activities and services in Nepal. Several manufacturing industries, including food processing, textiles, cement, brick production, distilleries, pulp and paper, and pharmaceuticals, rely on boilers to meet their thermal energy needs. Many large-scale short-term accommodation services and buildings, such as hotels, offices, and hospitals, also use boilers in Nepal. There are approximately 56,611 manufacturing establishments, and this sector employs more than 346,143 people in Nepal (CBS, 2022). Likewise, there are approximately 13,516 short-term accommodations, which employ about 35,658 people (NSO, 2024a). In fiscal year 2023/24, manufacturing industries contributed approximately 4.87% to GDP, while accommodation and food service activities contributed approximately 2.42% to GDP (NSO, 2024b). Both manufacturing industries and services are crucial to Nepal’s economy, supporting industrial growth, import substitution, job creation, and foreign exchange earnings.
<br> 
However, the country’s outdated boiler technologies and inefficient fuel use have resulted in high production costs, poor air quality, and an increasing dependency on imported fossil fuels. The industrial sector accounts for more than 30% of the country’s total final energy consumption (532.4 PJ) (WECS, 2024). These energy-intensive industries rely on a diverse mix of fuels — fuelwood, agricultural residues, diesel, furnace oil, gasoline, kerosene, LPG, coal, and electricity — for their boiler operations, each with varying costs, efficiencies, and environmental impacts. In 2023, coal accounted for the largest share (30%) in total industrial energy consumption, followed by fuelwood (29%), agricultural residues (15%), electricity (12%), diesel (11%), and others (furnace oil, gasoline, kerosene, and LPG) (3%) (WECS, 2024). Almost all of the country’s coal requirements are imported, with the industrial sector accounting for 99%. Furthermore, the average cost of energy use (including the purchase of fuels and electricity) in manufacturing industries is relatively high, accounting for approximately 7.5% of all input values. Although the share of the services sector in the country’s total final energy consumption is relatively small (5%), it relies mainly on traditional low-energy-content fuelwood (57%), followed by LPG (16%), solar (11%), electricity (10%), and others (5%).
<br>
Burning fossil fuels and traditional biomass with low energy content by industries and services for boiler operation contributes to air pollution and associated adverse health impacts. A recent study (World Bank, 2025) reported that air pollution is the number one risk factor for death and disability in Nepal, surpassing malnutrition (second) and tobacco (third). It reduces life expectancy by 3.4 years for the average Nepali and causes approximately 26,000 premature deaths annually. The economic cost of poor air quality is estimated to exceed 6% of Nepal’s GDP each year. Among these air pollutants, PM2.5 is the primary pollutant of concern for human health, and the report finds that PM2.5 concentrations are highest in the country’s capital, Kathmandu Valley, and the Terai region in the south. Approximately 20% of primary PM2.5 is attributed to emissions from industrial boilers in the Kathmandu Valley, and a similar percentage of locally generated secondary PM2.5 is linked to industrial activities in the Terai region (World Bank, 2025).
<br> 
It is projected that industrial fuel combustion in Nepal, led by boiler use, will increase significantly in the future. Industrial boilers are a critical component of Nepal’s manufacturing sector. However, limited empirical research has examined the economic performance, fuel cost competitiveness, and pollution control investment decisions associated with these boilers in the context of Nepal’s energy transition goals and environmental regulations. The Nepal Government has recognized the negative health impact of air pollution and has incorporated air quality improvement measures into its Sixteenth Five-Year Plan (NPC, 2025). Currently, Nepal has an excess electricity supply, especially during the monsoon season, and this surplus is expected to grow due to its rapidly expanding hydropower capacity. The Nepalese government is exploring new avenues to absorb this excess electricity supply. Paradoxically, Nepal has an excess supply of clean electricity; yet, most industrial boilers continue to rely on polluting biomass and imported fossil fuels, such as coal, petroleum products, and biomass, which pose serious health and environmental risks. This study aims to draw the attention of policymakers to address this critical policy failure by assessing the economic feasibility and environmental benefits of cleaner boilers in Nepal. This study aims to bridge the knowledge gap by examining the economics of industrial boiler systems in Nepal, with a focus on fuel costs, capital and operational expenditures, energy efficiency, and environmental compliance. The findings are particularly relevant in light of growing concerns about air pollution, rising fossil fuel import bills, and Nepal’s commitment to net-zero emissions by 2045.
